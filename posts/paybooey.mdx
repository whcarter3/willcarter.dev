I have a home server I built in my office, and it's named PayBooey in honor of my daughter Billie.

For anyone reading who is unaware, Billie died very suddenly and unexpectedly in August of 2024 from complications due to an undiagnosed tumor. I aim to talk more about her life on this blog in the future.

When Billie was learning to speak, she quickly caught on that we would play the Bluey theme song from our Google Home speaker with the phrase "Okay Google, Play Bluey." As toddlers do, she tried to initiate this herself, grasping the speaker with both hands and loudly commanding "okaayy gogo! pay BOOEY," which, to both mine and my wife's amazement, actually worked.

I built PayBooey after I got laid off. The idea was simple, if a little overdue: stop spending my whole career building on top of infrastructure I treated as someone else's department, and actually learn what was underneath it. I fell into the usual self-hosting rabbit hole almost immediately. One service turned into several, and to get any of them running I had to learn the parts of the stack I'd always waved my hand at: containers, ports, volumes, networking, disk and memory allocation, latency, the database services holding it all together...the list is endless.

Most of what runs on it is mundane and domestic, which is sort of the point:

- The media my family actually watches with the classic \*arr stack
- The retro game library I've been hauling around in one form or another since I was a kid myself with all of my own custom metadata and rating system. The perfect thing to stand in the way of ever finishing my backlog.
- The everyday stuff a family accumulates and would rather not lose to a billing change or a service quietly shutting down. r/datahoarders has a recent new subscriber

None of it is impressive on paper, and yet all of it is mine.

The surprise, to me, was how much I _really_ enjoyed the process. I'd braced for a chore, a tax I'd pay to feel a little less ignorant. Instead it turned into the most fun I've had with a computer in years, and somewhere in the mess I picked up a real, hands-on feel for the parts I used to hand off without a second thought.

I've also broken the whole thing and put it back together, which is its own kind of education. It's the homelab version of taking down prod with a bad config, except I get to just yolo to `main` here. One night I misconfigured DNS badly enough to take down the internet for the entire house, then spent the small hours fixing it before my son woke up and demanded his favorite music (which, of course, I haven't downloaded for offline play yet rendering it useless without our internet).

Thirteen years in, I'd let specialization harden into a kind of fence. The frontend was mine, and everything behind it felt like somewhere other people went, full of incantations I figured I wasn't equipped to read. Building PayBooey took that apart pretty fast. The stack underneath was never arcane. It was just unfamiliar, and I'd spent a long time letting unfamiliar stand in for impossible.

I won't pretend I came out of it a backend engineer. I came out of it with a server named for my daughter, running on a shelf in my office, and the quiet, useful fact that the thing I've built my career on top of isn't a mystery -- it's a stack, I can learn a stack -- and you know what? it's also pretty fucking cool. 💚
