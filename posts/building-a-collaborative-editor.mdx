The demo for a collaborative editor is the easy part. Two browser windows side by side, you type in one and the letters appear in the other. It looks like magic and it takes an afternoon. Then you try to ship it to real people doing real work, and you find out the demo was hiding almost everything that matters.

I spent a chunk of my time at Moment, a collaborative document editor, working on exactly this problem. Here is what the demo does not tell you.

The first hard question is what happens when two people edit the same spot at the same time. Person A deletes a word while Person B is italicizing it. Neither of them did anything wrong. The network delivered their changes in a different order to every client. So whose version is correct, and how do you make sure everyone converges on the same answer without a central referee blocking every keystroke?

There are two main schools of thought here. Operational Transformation, which is roughly what Google Docs grew up on, and Conflict-free Replicated Data Types, or CRDTs. OT keeps edits small and compact, but it pushes the hard work onto a server that has to transform every operation against every concurrent operation, and the transformation functions are famously easy to get subtly wrong. CRDTs flip the tradeoff. Each change carries enough identity with it that any two clients can merge in any order and land in the same state, no central authority required. You pay for that with metadata. Every character, in some designs, remembers who created it and where it sits relative to its neighbors.

We leaned toward the CRDT side, and the thing that surprised me was how much the rest of the editor had to bend around that choice. Your data model is not "a string of text" anymore. It is a structure of identified pieces, and every feature you build, comments, suggestions, formatting, has to speak that language.

Then there is presence, which sounds like a nice-to-have and is secretly a rabbit hole. Showing other people's cursors and selections in real time is table stakes now. The trouble is that a cursor is a position, and positions move when text changes. If your collaborator types three words above where my cursor is, my cursor needs to slide down to stay on the same logical spot, even though the raw character offset just changed underneath it. Map that wrong and people watch their cursor drift into the middle of someone else's sentence. Multiply it by selections, by remote highlights, and by the little name labels that float next to each caret, and "just show the cursors" becomes a real piece of engineering.

Offline is the next one. Someone opens the doc on a plane, edits for two hours with no signal, and then the wifi comes back. Now you have two hours of their changes and two hours of everyone else's changes, and they all have to merge into something coherent without losing work and without making the document look like it went through a blender. This is the case where CRDTs earn their keep, because merging is the thing they are built to do, but you still have to handle it gracefully in the UI so the person does not panic when the document reshuffles in front of them.

My personal favorite, the one I did not see coming, is undo. In a single-player editor, undo is obvious. You pressed a key, you press undo, the key un-presses. In a shared document, undo has to mean "undo the last thing I did," not "undo the last thing that happened." If my collaborator types a paragraph and I hit Cmd-Z, deleting their paragraph would be infuriating and, frankly, a little rude. So undo has to be scoped to each user's own actions, threaded through a history that other people are actively editing at the same time. It is a hard problem dressed up as a basic feature, and getting it to feel natural took far more care than the headline collaboration features did.

Performance hangs over all of it. A document that two people edit is easy. A document with twenty people, ten thousand paragraphs, and a few years of edit history is a different animal. You cannot keep every operation in memory forever, you cannot re-render the whole document on every keystroke, and you cannot let the metadata grow without bound. A lot of the unglamorous work is pruning, batching, and making sure the data structure stays fast as it ages.

If there is a lesson in any of this, it is that real-time collaboration is maybe ten percent the happy path and ninety percent the edge cases. The cursors-typing-in-sync demo is real, but it is the trailhead, not the destination. The actual work is in the moments where two people, two networks, and two slightly different versions of reality have to quietly agree on what the document says. When you get it right, nobody notices, which is the whole point.
